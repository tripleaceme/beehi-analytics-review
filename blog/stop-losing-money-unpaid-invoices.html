How to Track Unpaid Invoices and Follow Up on Payments

By BeeHi Analytics
Published 20 March 2026 · Updated 25 September 2026

To track unpaid invoices, keep one invoice register and a separate payment log. Give each invoice a unique ID, record its due date and subtract confirmed payments from the amount due. Review overdue balances regularly and record the next follow-up date.

This gives a laundry owner, stylist or repair business a reliable answer to “who still owes us?” without searching through messages. It also helps staff avoid chasing a customer who has already paid. A tracker supports collection; it cannot guarantee that an invoice will be paid.

Outstanding and overdue invoices are different

An outstanding invoice has an unpaid balance. An overdue invoice has an unpaid balance after its agreed due date. If payment is due on 30 September, the balance is still outstanding on 25 September but not yet overdue.

Use the agreed invoice terms to decide when to follow up. Do not treat every unpaid amount as a bad debt or assume every late payment is deliberate. The customer may be disputing the work, waiting for a corrected invoice or using a different payment reference.

Build your invoice register

Columns for an unpaid invoice tracker
ColumnWhat to record
Invoice IDA unique reference, such as INV-104
Customer and contactThe business or person responsible for payment
Issue date and due dateWhen the invoice was sent and payment is expected
Invoice amountThe amount billed
CreditsAgreed reductions, with a reason
Payments receivedConfirmed payments linked to this invoice
BalanceAmount billed minus credits and payments
Next actionFollow-up owner, date and latest note

Store contacts only where staff who need them can access them. Keep the original invoice and supporting records accessible from the register, rather than placing sensitive information in a publicly shared sheet.

Record partial payments without losing the history

Use a Payments tab with payment date, invoice ID, amount and bank or receipt reference. Add one row for each payment instead of overwriting the previous amount. This makes it easier to investigate a disputed balance.

Illustrative example: INV-104 is ₦120,000. The customer pays ₦50,000 on 10 September and ₦30,000 on 18 September. Total receipts are ₦80,000, leaving ₦40,000 outstanding. If an agreed ₦10,000 credit is later issued, the balance becomes ₦30,000.

If invoice IDs are in Payments column B and payment amounts in C, put an invoice ID in A2 of your register and use:

=SUMIF(Payments!B2:B1000,A2,Payments!C2:C1000)

Suppose the invoice amount is in D2, credits in E2 and the payment total in F2. The balance formula is =D2-E2-F2. A negative result needs review: it may be an overpayment or an incorrectly matched receipt. Do not hide it automatically.

Sort unpaid balances by how late they are

Use groups such as Not Yet Due, 1–7 Days Overdue, 8–30 Days and More Than 30 Days. These are useful review groups, not universal collection deadlines. Set your follow-up routine around your written payment terms and the customer’s circumstances.

With due date in C2 and balance in G2, this formula calculates days overdue for positive balances. A missing due date stays blank:

=IF(OR(C2="",G2<=0),"",MAX(0,TODAY()-C2))

Format due dates as dates, not text. Filter for positive balances before reviewing the ageing list. Separately flag missing due dates so those invoices do not disappear from your attention.

Use a repeatable follow-up routine

  1. Before sending: check the invoice amount, due date, contact and payment details.
  2. At the due date: reconcile recent receipts and confirm the invoice is still unpaid.
  3. When overdue: send a specific, polite reminder with the invoice reference and balance.
  4. After a reply: record any dispute or promised payment date and who owns the next step.
  5. On receipt: match the payment, update the balance and acknowledge it.

A simple payment reminder

Hello [name], a quick reminder that invoice [reference] was due on [date]. Our records show a balance of [amount]. Please let us know when we can expect payment, or send the payment reference if you have already paid. Thank you.

Agree any revised schedule in writing. Keep disputes separate from routine reminders so staff do not keep sending the same message while a problem is unresolved. The next step should reflect the facts, rather than an automatic threat or penalty.

Check receipts against the bank, not just messages

A screenshot is a useful lead for reconciliation, not the final record of money received. Check the business’s payment records before marking an invoice paid. Watch for one transfer covering several invoices, bank charges, duplicate entries and refunds.

Deposits and final payments should reduce the same invoice balance. A payment against an old invoice belongs in collections for the payment date; it is not automatically a new sale that day. Our guide to five small business numbers explains this distinction.

When a dashboard helps

A dashboard can show outstanding totals, overdue balances and customers to review, provided those fields are available and the records are current. It does not send reminders or collect payment unless those functions are specifically included. Compare the available views in our business dashboard examples.

Common questions about unpaid invoices

Should I delete an invoice once it is paid?

No. Keep its history and filter paid balances out of your active follow-up view. Deleting it makes future reconciliation harder.

Can one payment cover several invoices?

Yes. Allocate the payment across the correct invoice IDs and keep the common bank reference. The allocations should total the confirmed receipt.

Does an outstanding balance mean the business made a loss?

Not by itself. Unpaid sales and business profit answer different questions. Review the balance alongside cash received and costs rather than treating the invoice register as a full set of accounts.
