5 Numbers Every Small Business Owner Should Know

By BeeHi Analytics
Published 15 March 2026 · Updated 25 September 2026

The five numbers to start with are sales revenue, outstanding payments, orders in progress, overdue orders and gross profit margin. Together, they show what you sold, what customers still owe, what your team needs to deliver and how much is left after direct costs.

For a laundry, salon, repair shop or fashion business, these are useful daily operating checks. They do not replace a cash balance, a monthly profit review or proper accounts. Start with the last complete trading day, then compare like-for-like periods. All figures in the examples below are illustrative.

1. Sales revenue: what did you sell?

Add the value of sales recorded for the day, after discounts and returns. Use a consistent rule for when you record a sale. Keep the amount sold separate from the amount collected: an order worth ₦60,000 with a ₦20,000 deposit is not ₦60,000 of cash received.

Suppose a salon records ₦80,000 in services on Tuesday and ₦100,000 the following Tuesday. Sales are up 25%: the ₦20,000 increase divided by the earlier ₦80,000. Check whether a promotion, a longer working day or more appointments explains the difference before calling it a trend.

Record: sale date, transaction ID, service or product, sales amount and payment received. Compare a completed day with another completed day; comparing midday sales with yesterday’s full total can create a false alarm.

2. Outstanding payments: what do customers still owe?

For each invoice, subtract payments and agreed credits from the amount due. Keep deposits linked to their original invoice so they do not become a second sale. An unpaid invoice is outstanding; it becomes overdue only after its agreed due date.

A fashion business invoices ₦120,000 and receives ₦70,000. The outstanding balance is ₦50,000. If the balance is due on Friday, it should not appear as overdue on Wednesday. Record both the balance and due date, then review the overdue list before sending reminders.

Act on it: confirm the customer received the invoice, check whether payment has already arrived and agree the next follow-up. Our unpaid invoice tracking guide shows how to record partial payments and follow-ups.

3. Orders in progress: what work is still open?

Count orders that have started but are not complete. Also keep a separate view of accepted orders that have not started. Use clear statuses such as New, In Progress, Ready and Delivered so everyone records the same thing.

Ten alterations and ten wedding outfits create very different workloads. Alongside the order count, review the promised dates, assigned staff and estimated work remaining. A rising count can mean stronger demand or a bottleneck; the count alone cannot tell you which.

Act on it: sort open work by deadline. Check materials and staff availability before accepting a new delivery promise.

4. Overdue orders: what should already have been delivered?

Count orders whose promised delivery date has passed and whose status is not Delivered or Cancelled. Keep these separate from completed orders that were delivered late: one list needs action now, while the other helps you review reliability over time.

If a laundry has 18 open orders and three were due yesterday, those three belong at the top of the daily review. Confirm the reason for each delay, assign someone to resolve it and tell the customer the revised plan.

Record: promised date, current status and actual delivery date. Blank promised dates should appear as missing information, not automatically count as late.

5. Gross profit margin: what remains after direct costs?

Gross profit = sales revenue − direct cost of the goods or services sold. Gross profit margin is gross profit divided by sales revenue, expressed as a percentage. Match the costs to the sales in the same period.

For example, ₦100,000 of sales with ₦60,000 of direct costs leaves ₦40,000 gross profit and a 40% gross margin. Rent, administrative costs and other overheads still have to be covered. This is not the same as net profit or cash in the bank.

There is no single healthy margin for every business. Compare your own products, services and past periods. If job costs are incomplete, label the margin as an estimate and review it when the costs are known. If sales are zero, display “not available” rather than dividing by zero.

Set up a simple daily tracker

Start with one row per transaction or order, not a new tab for every day. Use real dates, numeric amounts and unique IDs. Keep payments in a separate log when an order can have multiple payments. For expense records, follow our Google Sheets expense tracker guide.

For a simple sales-only sheet with sale dates in A and amounts in B, this totals the date entered in D2, including dates that contain a time:

=SUMIFS(B2:B1000,A2:A1000,">="&D2,A2:A1000,"<"&D2+1)

For a status column in C, =COUNTIF(C2:C1000,"In Progress") counts matching orders. These formulas assume the stated columns and English function names; some spreadsheet locales use semicolons instead of commas. See Google’s SUMIFS documentation for the criteria syntax.

Make the numbers part of a daily routine

  1. Confirm yesterday’s entries and payments are complete.
  2. Compare sales with a similar day, not an arbitrary target.
  3. Review overdue balances and delivery promises.
  4. Assign a next action and owner to each exception.
  5. Review margins weekly if direct costs are not available daily.

A business dashboard can bring these checks together. It still depends on accurate records, the views included in your plan and when the data last refreshed.

Common questions about daily business numbers

Is revenue the same as money received?

No. A customer can pay later, pay a deposit or settle an older invoice. Track sales and collections separately so you can see both activity and available cash.

Do all five numbers need to be checked every day?

Review orders and overdue balances as often as your work requires. A weekly gross-margin review may be more useful when supplier or job costs arrive later. Choose a rhythm that supports decisions.

What should a business without customer orders track?

Adapt the operational measures. A gym may track active memberships and renewals due instead of delivery deadlines. Keep sales, collections and costs relevant to how that business works.
