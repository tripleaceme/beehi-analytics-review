How One Beauty Studio Found Its Hidden Profit Leak.

By Ayoade Adegbite · May 11, 2026 · 5 min read

The Problem

For years, Toluwalopekept her studio's records in notebooks like orders, payments, expenses, all of it. When she wanted to know which of her services brought in the most money for a given month, she'd open the book, run her finger down the page, and add the numbers up by hand. Same for a year. Same for any question worth asking.

It was workable, but it wasn't readable. She knew she was busy. She just couldn't tell where the money went or which work paid best. She'd tried writing a simple monthly summary herself, January's totals, February's totals and gave up halfway through. The tracking was costing her more attention than the answers were worth.

Then someone in her circle posted about BeeHi Analytics. That's how she got in touch.

The Solution

The first conversations were diagnostic: what records did she keep, how was she making pricing decisions, what was actually visible to her day-to-day. The honest answer: not much. Her books were essentially write-only.

We set her up the same way we did OlamideCrafts, a structured Google Sheet she updates from her phone, wired to a custom dashboard. Two sheets total: one for daily sales (name, date, service, amount, notes), one for operational expenses (the cashbook). The dashboard pulls from both automatically.

Updates take her a couple of minutes a day. The dashboard refreshes overnight, so every morning before she opens the studio the numbers are already waiting.

The Insight

The dashboard answered the questions she'd been trying to write down. But the bigger discovery was something she wasn't looking for.

  • The founder was eating the business's profit Toluwalopehad been profit-sharing herself on every job. A client paying ₦10,000 for a service meant ₦4,000 to her personally and ₦6,000 back to the business before expenses. Once we laid the cashbook (electricity, data, water for washing wigs, transport, materials) against revenue on the same screen, the math became obvious: at the end of the month, the business's ₦6,000 slice kept getting eaten by recurring expenses she didn't realise stacked that high. She wasn't paying herself but she was draining the business.
  • The fix: pay herself a salary, not a slice She didn't believe it at first. We went through the books together. The numbers held. She restructured her own pay into a fixed monthly salary, a number the business can actually afford, and started reinvesting the rest. She's since hired a salaried staff member on the same model.
  • Which services actually pay The Services tab shows revenue and profit margin per line, installations, revamps, new builds. She quotes the high-margin work first when her calendar gets tight, instead of taking whichever booking arrived loudest.
  • Who her top customers are A medal system tags every customer Gold / Silver / Bronze by lifetime spend. The top 10% of names get the personal follow-up; the rest get the standard booking flow.
  • What the business actually costs to run The recurring expense lines like data, electricity, water for washing wigs, transport, were each small. Added up monthly, they were the silent profit-killer. Now she sees them aggregated by category every time she opens the dashboard.

"I'd actually be sitting on way more net profit right now if I wasn't separating the money 🥲. And now I know exactly which items are taking the highest percentage of my monthly expenses."

— Toluwalope, founder of Styledbylulu

T still updates the sheet from her phone every day. The dashboard does the rest. She's making better pricing decisions, paying herself a real number she can plan around, and building a business that pays its founder and stays profitable for the first time.
